Ceramics experience, skills, and qualifications
Homer Watson House & Gallery — Open Studio application
2025–2026

I have been working in clay at Homer Watson House & Gallery since April 2025. In that time I have completed beginner and intermediate wheel courses, a six-week hand-building course, and an independent Open Studio season. I can work safely and independently in the Coach House: I centre my own clay, throw and trim my own forms, build by hand to a project-appropriate thickness, glaze my own pots, and monitor pieces through drying and firing.

Training

Relaxing Beginners Pottery Wheel with Alyssa Marie
4-week course, 2.5 hours per class
Saturdays, April 19, 26, May 10 & 17, 2025 (no class May 3)
10:00 am–12:30 pm, Coach House

This was my first structured time on the wheel. I learned studio safety, wedging, centering, opening, and pulling a wall. I began throwing simple cylinders and bowls, using the tools provided, and getting comfortable with wheel speed and water. The class was designed for absolute beginners; I used it to build a steady, repeatable throwing rhythm rather than rushing toward finished ware.

Relaxing Beginners Pottery Wheel with Alyssa Marie
6-week course, 2.5 hours per class
Saturdays, June 14, 21, 28, July 5, 12 & 19, 2025
10:00 am–12:30 pm, Coach House

A second beginner session gave me more hours on the wheel and more time with the same fundamentals. I practised centering more consistently, pulling taller and more even walls, and taking pieces through trimming and glazing. Repeating the beginner course was deliberate: I wanted the motions to be reliable before moving into a class that assumed prior wheel experience.

Cozy Pottery Wheel with Alyssa Marie
4-week course, 2 hours per class
Tuesdays, August 5, 12, 19 & 26, 2025
6:00–8:00 pm, Coach House
Experienced beginner to intermediate

This class was for potters who could already centre and pull a wall. I worked on throwing a bit bigger and taller, using wheel speed more intentionally, and choosing tools to support taller cylinders, bowls, and cups. The focus was control: even walls, a stable foot, and forms that survive drying, trimming, and firing. By the end of summer 2025 I was throwing functional cups, bowls, and small vessels independently.

Clay Explorations — Hand Building with Shannon
6-week course, 2 hours per class
Wednesdays, January 14, 21, 28, February 4, 11 & March 4, 2026

Alongside wheel work I took a dedicated hand-building course. I practised pinch, coil, and slab construction; joining at leather-hard; and keeping walls an even, project-appropriate thickness so pieces dry and fire without cracking. Hand-building is now part of my regular practice, not only a complement to throwing. Work from this period includes pinched and altered forms (a folded tri-lobe rim bowl; a pinched frog salt cellar with a small spoon) and sculptural additions on thrown bodies (modeled leaf attachments around a bowl rim; a heart finial on a lidded dish).

I held an Open Studio Access Pass for the winter season and worked independently during Thursday afternoon sessions. Wheel skills

I centre my own clay and throw off the hump or from a single ball, depending on the form. I can pull even side walls and have practised throwing taller cylinders and bowls, as taught in Cozy Pottery Wheel. Forms I throw regularly include:

- cups, yunomi, and tumblers
- mugs
- bowls in several profiles (open, rounded, midnight/dark-bodied)
- small plates and shallow dishes
- bud vases and low vessels
- a conical pour-over dripper with a matching mug
- a handled pitcher
- lidded jars (thrown body and lid, with a pulled or modeled knob)
- cylinders that I later carve or pair with a matching form (for example a carved wheat-stalk cylinder and letter holder)

I trim my own leather-hard ware: I can recentre a piece on the wheel, cut a foot ring, and leave an unglazed foot where the glaze design calls for it. I monitor thickness as I throw and again at the leather-hard stage so bowls and plates do not slump or crack. I attach handles I have pulled, and I fit lids by throwing lid and gallery to match.

Hand-building and altering

I construct pinched, coiled, and slab-built work to an even thickness and join additions at leather-hard with slip and scoring. Recent pieces include:

- a sculptural bowl with a thrown body and a wreath of modeled leaves at the rim
- a pinched frog salt cellar with painted eyes and a small spoon
- a folded, pinched tri-lobe rim on a bowl
- a garlic grater with a carved interior and rim holes
- a palm dish with a continuous carved spiral
- a lidded cream dish with a heart-shaped finial

I also alter thrown work: folding rims, carving at leather-hard (wheat-stalk relief, radiating teardrop texture, spirals), and adding sculptural elements without thinning the wall so far that the piece fails in the kiln.

Glazing and surface

I glaze my own pots. I use the underglazes and studio glazes provided at Homer Watson, and I plan interiors and exteriors separately when the form needs it (for example a pale-blue wave over a dark midnight bowl with a glossy navy well; a yunomi with olive-tan drip over cerulean and pooling in the interior). I have worked with:

- dipping and pouring
- reactive glazes (teal, cobalt, copper, oxblood, cream)
- speckle and blush surfaces (a pale-blue cup with rust blush and a tan rim; a moss-green cup)
- amber and chocolate earth-tone sets
- carved surfaces that read through a dark iron glaze
- leaving feet and rims unglazed where that is part of the design

I understand that glaze application, thickness, and how a piece sits in the kiln are my responsibility. I do not leave glaze on foot rings.

Studio practice and independence

Open Studio at Homer Watson is a good fit for how I already work. I can:

- work safely and independently in the Coach House
- centre, throw, and trim without one-to-one instruction
- keep hand-built walls an appropriate thickness
- glaze my own work
- monitor drying so pieces are not rushed onto the kiln shelf wet, and not left so dry that attachments fail
- clean my wheel, tools, and table, and follow studio flow around shared bats, ware boards, and kiln loads

I treat the studio as a shared workspace: I wedge only what I will use, I label work, and I plan projects that fit a weekly afternoon session plus the time between Thursdays for drying and trimming.

Body of work (2025–2026)

The attached portfolio is a scrapbook of recent finished ware, photographed as fired. It includes an earth-tone collection (carved cylinder, lidded jar, bowls and dishes in amber and chocolate); a pour-over dripper and mug in reactive teal and copper; a mug and plate used together; marbled oxblood-and-cream bowl and plate; bud vases; yunomi; a midnight bowl; a green pitcher and small cups; a sculptural leaf-rimmed bowl; a frog salt cellar; a spiral black dish and garlic grater; a folded-rim bowl; and leather-hard carving on the wheel. The work is wheel-thrown and hand-built stoneware, mostly functional, with some sculptural rims and carved surfaces.

I am applying for Open Studio so I can keep that independent practice: throwing a little larger, refining lids and handles, and continuing hand-built and carved work without needing a taught class each season.
